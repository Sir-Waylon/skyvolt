# circuit-lab-b / static: Charge & Coulomb  (151 questions, 35 families)
## STUDY NOTE (what students are taught)
Two kinds of charge. Like charges repel; opposite charges attract. Electrons are the mobile charges in solids. Charge is conserved and quantized: one electron is −1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C. Field lines point away from + and toward −, in the direction of force on a positive test charge. Field strength E = F/q. Coulomb's Law: F = k·q₁q₂/r². Use meters and coulombs (1 µC = 10⁻⁶ C, 1 nC = 10⁻⁹ C, 1 cm = 0.01 m). The sign only tells you attract or repel. Worked example. +3.0 µC and +5.0 µC, 0.30 m apart: F = (8.99×10⁹)(3.0×10⁻⁶)(5.0×10⁻⁶)/(0.30)² = 0.1349/0.090 = 1.50 N, repulsive. Scaling: force ∝ q₁q₂ and ∝ 1/r². Triple r → 1/9. Double one charge and double r → 2/4 = ½. Charging and polarization Friction (triboelectric): electrons move from one insulator to the other. Glass rubbed with silk: glass loses electrons and becomes positive. Conduction: touching a charged object shares charge, giving the same sign. Induction: a charged rod near (not touching) a neutral conductor moves charge; grounding the far side and removing the ground before the rod leaves a net charge of the opposite sign. Polarization: a neutral insulator (paper, wall) shifts its charges slightly. The nearer opposite charge pulls harder than the farther like charge pushes, so a charged balloon sticks to a neutral wall. Static hazards and control Static has voltage but no continuous current. Sparks can ignite fuel vapor or dust. Electrostatic discharge (ESD) can destroy chips. Dry air builds charge; humid air bleeds it away. Grounding, wrist straps, antistatic mats and bags drain charge safely.
